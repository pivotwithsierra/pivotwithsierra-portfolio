From "what's for dinner?" to a product I could defend

How I turned an everyday family problem into a researched, evaluated AI product concept, and what I did when the AI got it wrong.

The short version: I identified a meaningful problem, investigated it, translated it into clear AI instructions, evaluated the results, and guided the work toward a useful product.
ChatGPT · synthesis partner Copilot · enterprise thinking partner Gemini NotebookLM · research partner Claude · independent reviewer Lovable · builder
01

Why this problem

I'm a mother and a chef. In my house, grocery shopping is never one decision. It's money, time, everyone's preferences, the kids, what's already in the fridge, and the real question: what will everyone actually eat?

Most tools solve one piece. A list app doesn't know when payday is. A budget app doesn't know my kids won't touch mushrooms. A store's app wants me to buy more. I wanted to know whether a planner could hold all of it at once, and whether AI could help me build it responsibly.

My working philosophy carried into this project: technology doesn't create transformation, people do. So I started with the people and the decision they face, not with the technology.

For the family: the goal was never a smarter app. It was a mom feeling sure her family will eat well until the next paycheck.
PromptMy first brief, before any research

My opening request to ChatGPT described the whole wish list at once:

"I want the app budget based on their set spending limit, how often they get paid, have the option to pull all store sales ads within up to a 50 mile radius … it should know favorites of the household … it should be able to create most cost effective shopping plan as a personal shopper along with time it will take to get all shopping/pickups done."

Excerpt, lightly corrected for spelling. Source: my original ChatGPT conversation.

I then asked for professional feedback, not flattery: build needs, risk, and what it would take to launch.

02

Research: separating what's true from what sounds true

My first idea tried to solve seven problems at once. Before building anything, I narrowed it to one defined question and tested every claim around it.

The defined problem: "Will my groceries make it to payday?" for busy moms, single parents and women with young children.

I ran the research in source-grounded notebooks with explicit rules: label every claim PROVEN, SUPPORTED or UNPROVEN, cite the source, and say "Not in sources" instead of guessing.

Five lines I didn't let blur

Assumption vs. fact

The 10–12% savings target came from my own planning. I relabeled it a founder assumption until receipts prove otherwise.

Documentation vs. marketing

I replaced competitor "alternatives" blogs with Mealime's own shutdown notice, and dropped a low-quality market-size report.

Possible vs. accessible

An Instacart integration is technically possible; it still needs an application and approval I don't have.

Proposed price vs. willingness to pay

$6.99/month was a hypothesis. My rubric counts only real payments or sponsor commitments, not "I would pay."

Sample vs. measured

Every dashboard number in the prototype is labeled sample data. Nothing here is a pilot result.

FindingThe research that changed the product

The notebook found that food insecurity in this group is driven mainly by income and prices, not budgeting mistakes. Software can't create money. That pushed two changes:

1. The outcome moved from "dollars saved" to "did your food last until payday?", a question families can answer even when income doesn't change.
2. The product added connections to food help families may be missing (WIC, school meals, pantries), which adds resources instead of only optimizing them.

For the family: I wouldn't promise a mom savings she can't get. I'd promise a plan that stretches what she has, and help finding more.

03

Prompting: giving the AI rules, not wishes

I treated each AI tool as a team member with a role, and gave it context, constraints, a required output format and a way to admit uncertainty.

Context

Who it's for, the positioning, and what was already decided, so answers built on the plan instead of restarting it.

Constraints

Label every claim; cite sources; no invented numbers; cut features stay cut.

Output shape

Tables, rankings and rubrics I could check, ending with "the single biggest weakness in your own recommendation."

Sequencing

One prompt at a time. Each answer was reviewed before the next prompt went out.

PromptSetting the rules for Copilot (my enterprise thinking partner)
"You're my startup advisor for a family grocery planning venture. Act as a skeptical product strategist and investor, not a cheerleader. Rules for every answer: - Label each claim as VERIFIED (with a source link), ESTIMATE, or OPINION. - If you don't know something, say so instead of guessing. - End each answer with the single biggest weakness in your own recommendation."

Prompt 1 of 15 in the Copilot planning record. Prompts were drafted with Claude as a thinking partner; I set the direction, sequenced and sent them.

DirectionMy own steering, in my words

These are the instructions I gave while shaping the prompts (lightly corrected for spelling):

"Identify the top 3 problems this website/app will solve. NO ideas. I need 10 solutions for each problem."
"My target audience are busy moms, single parents, women with SMALL kids, NOT JUST FAMILIES."
"DO NOT LIST OUT THE PRODUCTS. ONLY list the digital products trending for that solution."
"WE NEED PROVEN AND SOURCED, fact-driven research. It also needs to address our rubric, act as investor and show us the gaps and what would truly differentiate us."
ResponseAn answer worth keeping
"The core asset is not grocery planning. The core asset is: helping a mom feel confident that her food budget will make it to payday without carrying the entire mental load herself."

Copilot, Prompt 11 (five guiding questions). I kept this framing and tested it against research before adopting it.

PromptRules for the builder (Lovable project brief)
"All data is SAMPLE data. … Allergies are hard filters: never show a meal containing a listed allergen. … Do NOT build: multi-store price comparison, pantry inventory tracking, delivery ordering, store account connections, or automatic purchasing."

Excerpt from the Plenty project brief loaded before any build.

For the family: clear rules up front meant the AI couldn't quietly turn a mom's planner into a store's sales tool.

04

Evaluation: what a good answer looks like

I scored AI responses against seven questions. Each one below is paired with a real response that failed it during this project.

1. Does it address the household's actual need?
Failed: plans promised 10–12% grocery savings to families already spending near the federal minimum.
2. Does it follow the stated constraints?
Failed: a research summary brought back "multi-store, pantry-first" planning, two features I had cut.
3. Are calculations correct?
Failed: a rubric score of 91.7 counted national statistics as if they were interviews with local moms.
4. Are claims supported?
Failed: a "60–70% churn" figure appeared three times with no real source, and a sponsor model was labeled PROVEN with no sponsor conversations.
5. Does it identify missing information?
Failed: grant plans skipped eligibility; the key foundations fund 501(c)(3) nonprofits only.
6. Is the language clear, useful and respectful?
Revised: "eliminated" works on TV, not for a 3-year-old at dinner. The game now offers a bonus round instead.
7. Does it preserve the intended product scope?
Failed: a plan recommended human-assisted operations, the labor cost that shut down a competitor.
CritiqueWhy the 91.7 score was rejected

The rubric defines most Gate 1 criteria by field evidence ("X of 40 interviews," "sponsors request follow-up"). The notebook scored them from national data. Rescored honestly, desk research could support only about 49% of the gate, at roughly 62/100, with zero sponsor conversations scoring 1 of 5.

That's the expected position before field research, and it set up the next phase instead of a false "Go."

For the family: a confident wrong answer is worse than "we don't know yet." The rubric kept the project honest about both.

05

Refinement: output, critique, new instruction, better result

I ran a cross-model review: Copilot drafted, NotebookLM researched, Claude reviewed the outputs at my direction, and I made the calls.

Documented example: the venture rubric

1 · Output

Rubric v1: six weighted sections in one score, mixing before-launch guesses with after-pilot results.

2 · Critique

No money criterion, no allergy-safety stop, criteria counted twice, and scores described as feelings ("some focus").

3 · Revised instruction

"Split it into two gates … Add a willingness-to-pay / sponsor criterion … Add an allergy-safety kill criterion … Rewrite every 1/3/5 description as a number or observable evidence."

4 · Result

Rubric v2.0: Gate 1 before building, Gate 2 after a 90-day pilot, with kill criteria including "any allergen recommendation pauses the pilot."

PromptThe full correction instruction
"Revise the master rubric with these fixes: 1. Split it into two gates. Gate 1 (before building) … Gate 2 (after a 90-day, 50-user pilot) … 2. Add a willingness-to-pay / sponsor criterion and a kill criterion for no payment or sponsor evidence. 3. Add an allergy-safety kill criterion: any known allergen violation stops the pilot until fixed. 4. Remove double counting … 5. Rewrite every 1/3/5 description as a number or observable evidence. 6. Add the missing MVP metrics … 7. Only receipt-backed savings may be shown to users. 8. Add research stopping rules, a one-page scorecard, and the 3 criteria most likely to fail."

More course corrections

Business model

SNAP-Ed removed as a funder

The notebook ranked it a top sponsor. I verified the program's federal funding had ended in 2025 and removed it.

Safety

Allergy filter fixed in the build plan

The first build plan filtered peanuts only. The corrected plan excludes peanuts and mushrooms through one shared filter, with tests for both.

Brand

"Chopped at Home" became Freezer Dash

My game idea kept its spirit and dropped a TV show's trademarked name.

Design

First homepage rejected

The first generated homepage didn't fit the brand. I reset the direction to warm cream and plum.

NoteAbout "AI training"

I did not train or fine-tune a model. My work was prompt design, response evaluation, rubric development and iterative refinement: directing model behavior through instructions, examples, evaluation criteria and feedback, and verifying claims against primary sources.

For the family: every correction moved the product closer to what a tired mom needs on a Tuesday night, and further from what's easy to build.

Meet Plenty

Plenty is a free, text-first grocery planner that helps busy moms make their food money last until payday, and points them to food help they may be missing. Community partners pay for it. Stores never do.

Tagline: Peace of mind between paydays. Its main feature, Payday Peace of Mind, plans around the pay cycle instead of a single shopping trip.

ProcessHow I specified and checked this interaction

Where it started. Freezer Dash came from my kitchen, not from a model. My first description of the game:

one veggie, 1 starch!… or they will be eliminated

As a chef, I'd already worked out what to do with a real basket of pizza rolls, mixed vegetables and chicken wings:

cut the pizza rolls open get the red sauce out to put in the mixed veggies… crouton with the leftover dough!

And the grown-up round:

they can only add to the meal things that are expiring before the next pay day

The build spec I wrote (summarized from my instructions):

  1. Choose your crew: sample family members with their food exclusions and age-appropriate jobs.
  2. A working 7-minute countdown with Start, Pause, Resume and Restart.
  3. Build a basket across Veggie, Starch, Protein and Extra. Add and remove items, with category indicators that update immediately.
  4. Judges' table with explicit rules. Missing a veggie or starch earns a friendly 1-minute bonus round.
  5. Use-It-Up: a grown-up round where only items dated before the next sample payday qualify. Label the dates as demo assumptions, and don't imply a date alone proves food is safe.
  6. Tonight's dinner: rule-based templates matched to the actual basket. If nothing fits, explain what's missing. Never the same dinner regardless of picks.
  7. Jobs and badges: knife, heat and raw-meat tasks labeled grown-up jobs. Badges only when their stated conditions are met.

Plus: apply the crew's allergies and hard-nos everywhere, say plainly that sample filtering can't verify real labels or cross-contact, keep state for this session only, and offer a labeled Quick demo.

Checks completed

  • Passed: 16 automated rule tests in Node 22 (crew needs a grown-up; allergy and hard-no blocking; timer start, pause, resume, restart and time-up; add, remove and pick limits; judges and a single bonus round limited to the missing categories; payday cutoff and grown-up-only Use-It-Up; my original basket returns the Pizza Party Sheet Pan; 7 different baskets return 7 different dinners; no-match explanation; excluded items never reach a dinner; knife, heat and raw-meat steps never go to a kid; each badge awarded only on its condition).
  • Passed: 4 scripted click-throughs of this page in a simulated browser (jsdom): the full game with a bonus round, Quick demo, the clock running out and sending the basket to the judges, and Restart clearing the round.
  • Passed: 13 scripted checks in real headless Chrome: the game opens, the clock really counts down and freezes on Pause, judges, dinner, Escape to close, opening from a direct link, keyboard-only play from crew to judges, a 390px phone screen with no sideways scrolling, and reduced-motion mode.
  • Still needs testing: hands-on play on real phones and in Safari and Firefox, screen readers, timer accuracy when the tab is in the background, and play-testing with real families. All of these checks were run by script; nobody has play-tested it yet.
BusinessThe SaaS model behind Plenty (hypothesis)

B2B2C: families use Plenty free. The paying customer is a partner organization, such as a family resource center, food bank, foundation or employer. Partners enroll families and see anonymized outcomes in the Partner View. Proposed pricing is a per-enrolled-family monthly fee, often paid from grant funding. This is untested: no partner has signed.

NextHow it would be validated

Gate 1 (before building further): 40 interviews across two segments, a 100-visitor landing-page test, and at least 5 sponsor conversations. Gate 2 (after a 90-day pilot): food lasting until payday, pay-cycle retention, budget accuracy against receipts, and zero allergy violations.
